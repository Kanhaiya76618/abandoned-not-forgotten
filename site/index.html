The story

Nothing gets tidied up out there

On Earth, the things we leave behind get buried, rust away or wash off in the rain. On the Moon there is no wind and no rain. Boot prints and rover tracks from the 1970s are still there, and NASA’s Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter has photographed them from orbit.

Since the 1960s, space agencies have sent machines to the Moon, to Mars and far beyond. When a mission ends, the machine stays where it is. Some are silent. A few are lost. And a few are still working.

Each one has a last known position. Let’s go and visit them.

The Moon · 384,400 km away

A museum with no visitors

Six times between 1969 and 1972, astronauts landed on the Moon. Each time they flew home in the top half of their lunar module and left the bottom half behind, along with cameras, tools, science stations and, on the last three trips, a car.

Robots got there first, and robots kept going. Here is what is still on the Moon.

    Mars · 55–401 million km away

    The ghost rovers

    Mars is harder. It has dust storms, freezing nights and air too thin to breathe, and it is so far away that radio signals take minutes to arrive. Even so, NASA has landed nine missions there.

    Most of the machines here were built to last a few months. Many lasted years. Their stories tend to end the same way: with a signal that never came back.

      Deep space · from next door to billions of km away

      Messages in a bottle

      Some machines never land anywhere. Pioneer 10 and 11, Voyager 1 and 2, and New Horizons were flung so fast that the Sun’s gravity will never pull them back. They are leaving for good.

      Because they could drift for millions of years, some carry messages: a gold plaque on each Pioneer, and on each Voyager a golden record of sounds and pictures from Earth.

      Others never left, and ended up parked in stranger places. Dawn still circles the dwarf planet Ceres with empty fuel tanks. NEAR Shoemaker rests on the asteroid Eros. The Kepler telescope follows Earth around the Sun, switched off.

            The silence board

            Some missions end with a goodbye. Most just go quiet. Teams keep listening for weeks or months, sometimes sending hundreds of wake-up calls, before they decide a spacecraft is gone. These counters run live.

                About this story

                What will we leave next?

                NASA’s Artemis program plans to send astronauts back to the Moon. Curiosity and Perseverance are exploring Mars right now. One day they will fall silent too.

                The machines in this story showed us that the Moon is slowly drifting away, that Mars once had water, and what lies at the edge of the Sun’s bubble. They are abandoned, but not forgotten.

                For teachers: three classroom activities

                1. Ghost hunt · 15 min. Use the Moon map to find all three Apollo rovers and Lunokhod 2. Which one drove farthest? Who broke its record, and where?
                2. Silence math · 10 min. Use the silence board to find how many days Opportunity has been silent. A sol is 1.0275 Earth days. How many sols is that?
                3. Write a mission log · 20 min. Pick a machine and write its last day in the first person, using at least three real facts from its dossier.

                  Sources & method

                  Every fact in a dossier links to its source, NASA or JPL wherever possible. Moon positions come from LROC’s Coordinates of Lunar Anthropogenic Features. Mars positions are published landing coordinates. The simulator and the Light Clock place the planets with JPL’s approximate planetary positions and the spacecraft with JPL Horizons trajectories. Ceres, Eros and the Kepler telescope follow orbits from JPL Horizons too; the map of Ceres is from NASA’s Dawn mission (shown in grey), and the shape of Eros is a sketch. Map imagery: NASA Trek and NASA’s CGI Moon Kit. Planet textures other than the Moon and Mars: Solar System Scope (CC BY 4.0). Photos are public-domain NASA images, credited in each dossier. The machines’ first-person logs are imagined, written from real mission facts, and always labeled.
